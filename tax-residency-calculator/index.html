What is the 183-day rule?

Many countries treat someone who spends 183 days or more there in a tax year, or in a twelve-month period, as tax resident. It is a rule of thumb, not the whole test: ties such as a home, family, work and where your life is centred can make you resident with fewer days, and a treaty between two countries decides when both claim you.

Calendar year or any twelve months?

It depends on the country. Some count the calendar year; others count any twelve consecutive months, so 183 days can be reached across New Year. Russia counts 183 days in twelve consecutive months, with the final status settled for the calendar year; Georgia counts any continuous twelve months that end in the tax year. The UK and Australia run their own tax years. Pick the period your country uses.

Do the days of arrival and departure count?

Here, yes: every day you were in the country counts as a day, as most countries count them. Not all do — the UK counts the days you were there at midnight, and the US uses its own weighted three-year test — so check how your country counts before relying on a close number.

Why does the number include trips I have not taken yet?

For a tax year, a trip you plan later in the same year counts towards it, so the calculator shows the whole year. For any twelve months, it counts the twelve months ending on the day you check.

Rules as checked on 5 November 2026. They change — the country's foreign ministry or embassy has the final word, and this page is a calculator, not legal advice.
