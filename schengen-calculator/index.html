How the 90/180 rule works

On any day you are in the Schengen area, look back over the 180 days ending that day. You may have spent at most 90 of them in the area. The window moves every day, so days come back one at a time as old ones fall out of the back of it — there is no date on which the count resets.

Do the days of entry and exit count?

Yes. The day you arrive and the day you leave each count as a full day, even if you were there for an hour. A weekend from Friday to Sunday is three days.

Which countries share the 90 days?

All 29 Schengen states share one allowance: Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Iceland, Italy, Latvia, Liechtenstein, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden and Switzerland. Monaco, San Marino and the Vatican have no border with them, so days there count too. Cyprus and Ireland are not in Schengen and have rules of their own.

I have a residence permit in one Schengen country

Days in the country that issued your residence permit or long-stay national visa do not count — leave them out of the trips above. In the other Schengen countries you are a short-stay visitor, and 90/180 applies there as it does to anyone.

Will a border officer actually know?

Yes. The EU Entry/Exit System records every entry and exit of non-EU travellers electronically, so an overstay is calculated by the system rather than read from passport stamps. An overstay can mean a fine, a record that follows you to every later visa application, and a ban on entry.

What this calculator does not know

Some nationalities have bilateral agreements with individual Schengen states that allow longer stays there, and a visa can carry its own shorter limit. When the answer matters, check it against the visa itself and the rules of the country you are going to. This page is a calculator, not legal advice.
